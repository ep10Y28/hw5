Reflection: what our agent team actually did, and what we'd change

Everything below is drawn from our full run on 2026-11-07 (shop date 2026-08-31): the Actual sections on the Ticket 101/102/103 tabs, the Cash tab, output/audit_trail.json, and the working database after the run. Three tickets, five agents, one model (gpt-6-luna), $3,400 in, $160 left.

1. How we'd evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why

For a shop like Campus Customs, "the board says resolved" is not good enough. Ticket 101 is the proof: it's stamped RESOLVED, but Tauhid still doesn't have a tee. So we grade every run on six things, in priority order. The first two cost real money if they go wrong, so they're pass/fail. The rest are graded.

LensWhat we checkWhy it matters at this shop
1. Money safety (pass/fail)No payment without a human signature. No balance below zero. Amounts come from the DB, not the model.Checking only had $3,400 and cash only goes out. A single wrong payment could cost us the rent.
2. Facts are true (pass/fail)Every number the agents quote matches the DB: stock, invoice, lease, margin.A made-up ETA or price ends up in a customer draft, and that's a broken promise to a real student.
3. Right routingOnly the specialists the ticket needs. Who went first, and was that the right first question?Each extra agent costs tokens and time, and sending everyone to everything is the failure the assignment warns about.
4. Business outcomeDid the ticket actually move toward done for the customer or landlord? What's left for a person, and is that honest?The shop's goal is a tee in Tauhid's hands and rent paid on time, not a tidy status field.
5. Communication qualityIs the customer draft useful, honest and free of internal detail (no "we owe our printer $840")?Drafts are the only thing a customer ever sees from this whole system.
6. Cost and speedModel requests, tool calls, tokens and seconds from the audit trail. Any duplicate work?Multi-agent chats burn tokens fast, and the course caps us at one model for exactly that reason.

Why grade it this way: the two pass/fail lenses are where the agents were strongest. That's because those rules live in the MCP tools (no amount argument, the cash reserve check, the human-only pay tool), not in the prompts, so there were 0 guardrail blocks and 0 unapproved dollars. The graded lenses are where the agents fell short: routing order, following through and communication. Those depend on the model's judgment.

How we'd keep evaluating: reset, re-run the same three tickets several times, and automatically score each run against SQL ground truth (stock, invoice, lease, cash), the audit trail (requests, tokens, duplicate hand-offs) and the end state (cash_accounts = $160, payments = #1 + #2). One good run can be luck. Ten consistent runs is evidence.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why

  • Ticket 102, clearly. It's a straight line: read lease 1, check cash, queue rent. One agent with get_lease_rent_due, get_cash_position, request_payment and post_to_board needs about 6 tool calls. The team used 12, including get_lease_rent_due twice (once by Facilities, again by Accounting), plus 13 model requests across three agents. Facilities' whole contribution was one tool call that Accounting then repeated. The hand-off added cost and no judgment.
  • Ticket 101, mostly. The reasoning is one chain: no stock → which vendor → vendor blocked by 501 → can we afford 501 → tell the customer what we know. Splitting it across three parallel agents is exactly what broke the chain. One agent would have written Tauhid's draft after learning the 5-day lead time, and could have queued the $8 tee PO in the same pass.
  • The status bookkeeping. get_ticket → update_ticket_status → post_to_board → resolve happened on every ticket. That's a script, not a team.
  • Where the team earned its keep:
    • Separation of duties. Only Accounting can queue money and only Customer Service can write customer drafts, enforced by per-agent allowlists and stamp_and_guard. A real shop wants the person who finds a bill not to be the same one who pays it.
    • Readable audit trail. "Accounting queued #1" is something a manager can audit.
    • Parallel, independent checks. On 103, stock and margin really are independent.
    Even so, most of our safety came from the MCP tools, not from having five agents. One agent with the same tools would still have been unable to pay without Eddie or to overdraw. Our honest take: for three tickets this size, one well-prompted agent with our 17 MCP tools would be simpler and cheaper. The team structure pays off when tickets need different expertise at the same time, or when we want hard role boundaries for compliance.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve, and what we'd need to build

  1. October rent with $160 in the bank, because we can't bring money in. Lease 1's next_due is now 2026-10-02 for $2,400, and checking is $160. Today the team can only say no: request_payment refuses, and Boss can mark the ticket blocked. Nothing in the app records a sale, a deposit or a customer payment. Cash only goes out (see the Cash tab). Even the Yale AI Club deal ($417.60 for 8, or $1,044 for all 20 at 10%) would never show up in cash_accounts.
    We'd need:
    • MCP tools record_sale(ticket_id, sku, size, qty, unit_price), which writes a new sales table and decrements inventory.
    • record_customer_payment(sale_id, amount, method), human-confirmed, which raises the checking balance.
    • forecast_cash(days), which projects the balance from leases, open invoices and expected sales.
    • A new Sales & Collections agent to turn quotes into confirmed orders and chase deposits.
    • Accounting gets the forecast, so it would say "rent is covered only if the club pays its deposit by 9/25".
  2. Receiving the restock and handing over the product. If Eddie approves a PO, execute_approved_payment pays the vendor, but inventory never changes. Nothing records Bulldog's delivery on 9/05, nothing reserves Tauhid's tee, and nothing marks it picked up. Drafts #2 and #11 also never leave the board, and the PO itself is never sent to Bulldog.
    We'd need:
    • receive_shipment(po_id, qty_received), which increases inventory.qty and flags short shipments.
    • reserve_stock(ticket_id, sku, size, qty) and fulfill_order(ticket_id), so the 8 hoodies are held for the club and not sold twice.
    • send_message(board_id) and transmit_purchase_order(po_id), both gated behind a human approval just like payments.
    • A Fulfillment / Receiving agent that owns the shelf.
    • Customer Service would gain "send after approval" instead of "draft only".
  3. Follow-up over time. The shop's clock is frozen at desk.date_today = 2026-08-31, and a ticket is only worked when someone clicks Run. That's why 101's "order the tee after #1 is paid" item just sits in Boss's needs_human list, and why nobody will notice on 9/05 if Bulldog doesn't deliver, or on 9/28 that October rent is coming.
    We'd need:
    • Tools advance_day() or a real clock, create_followup(ticket_id, due_date, reason), list_due_followups() and reopen_ticket(ticket_id).
    • A Dispatcher / Ops agent, run on a schedule, that wakes up each shop day, re-opens tickets whose blockers cleared (like 101 after #1 was paid), chases overdue ETAs and raises rent alerts a week ahead.
    • On the dashboard, a "Follow-ups today" column next to "Needs your signature".

Bottom line

The money rules held perfectly: two payments, both signed by Eddie, and $3,400 − $840 − $2,400 = $160 matches cash_accounts. That's because we put those rules in the tools, not in the prompts. Where the team fell short was follow-through (101's tee never ordered, 103's offer never made) and coordination cost (fan-out on 101 and a duplicate Inventory run on 103). The next build is clear: a sequential-when-dependent rule for Boss, then revenue, receiving and follow-up tools, so a "resolved" ticket means the student actually got their hoodie.
